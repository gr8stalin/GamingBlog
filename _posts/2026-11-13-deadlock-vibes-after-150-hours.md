Two years ago I [disavowed Deadlock on the premise that it's a MOBA](https://x.com/MustacheStalin/status/1822409463589904693). I recently relented and put some time into it. By some, I mean 150-ish hours.

I qualified Infernus, Abrams, Rat King, and Haze for ranked and currently trying to get Paige and Dynamo qualified as well. I have a few opinions that I've been able to put into words, and while overall I think there's definitely something there in Deadlock, it's still a MOBA at the end of the day.

# I really like the movement system

It's a Source (2) game, and after 15+ years spread between various arena shooters, being able to play a game with air strafing again made me feel like I was home. It's taking time to get used to Deadlock's unique movement options and mechanics like chaining slides for temporary infinite ammo, walljumps to cover distance, warp stone instant air dashes, the list goes on.

When I land from a zipline, take off from a launch pad, or otherwise find myself airborne, it feels right. Natural. I missed this so much, exactly as much as I missed micro & macro when I booted up Age of Empires 4 ranked. Fighting games could *never* ***ever*** compete.

I have to admit that there might be a little **too** much in that movement skill creates way too much of an insurmountable disparity between the mover and their opponent. 

Technically you can close the gap with stuff like Knockdown, Slowing Hex, etc., and you don't really ***need*** all of the really crazy movement tech to get the most out of the game.

My beef is that if you decide to take Deadlock seriously (i.e., you start scrimming/competing) I think it's an insurmountable gap if you are employed or care about your studies/future unless you dedicate a particularly outsized chunk of your free time to practicing for an hour or so per day on top of actually playing and doing other things that make you a well-rounded person (see footnote)[^1].

# Valve masterclass in arts

I always liked Deadlock's characters, the visual design of the world, and the music they concocted. After going through all the dev commentary across Half Life, Portal, and Team Fortress 2 years ago, I'm convinced that Deadlock is a masterclass demonstration of the time and effort required to make a game worth engaging with.

When presented with a character from Valorant or Overwatch, they're mostly a mishmosh of cliches and stereotypes meant to fuel a Quip Machine instead of a real person. They exist as containers for gameplay functions, and any richness or depth to them is artificial, stapled on by external and entirely separate 3DCG cutscenes.

Then you listen to [three conversations between Paige and Seven](https://deadlock.vlviewer.com/conversations/216a0677f0271afb38eb5b0e15b61ca2/) and realize that even in these chats that can mostly be ignored on the zipline ride to the lane, these are deep personages, people who exist and lead lives in the world of Deadlock's Cursed Apple.

I obviously didn't play Deadlock before the massive detailing overhaul in the City Never Sleeps update, but looking at videos of what people were playing on versus what I see in my games, Valve's ability to make even geometry appealing is unmatched.

# For a MOBA, it's actually pretty engaging

There's a surprising amount of flexibility and activity in Deadlock. All the DotA-isms like denying and last hitting are there and feel better to do, and the movement + camera angle + control scheme doesn't mean you have the sluggish MOBA controls.

The lack of early movement speed and generally limited arena traversal options in traditional MOBAs are in part to balance the game; it takes time and commitment for a team to decide to not show up to their lanes for the opportunity to score a free kill on a jungler on the other team at game start, and if the group is either not fast or committed enough to the plan, it fails.

The thing is, the lack of movement and limited traversal feels awful if you've played literally anything else. In RTS games, speed and limited traversal is offset by the satisfaction of multiple concurrent actions. In FPS games, the speed and traversal abilities are present for all players. In fighting games, regardless of the speed of a given character, it's mostly offset by the limited size of the arena.

As a consequence of the commitment behind movement, laning is a snoozefest in traditional MOBAs (DotA, League, etc.). Sometimes it gets a little spicy when the lane tries some positioning to get a early kill, but most of the lane is about last hitting, denying, and responsibilities divided between the carry and support like harassment and creep pulling. Everything is a commitment so something has to give in terms of risk mitigation.

In Deadlock, the freedom of movement makes games more active. There's more positioning, more rotating, more of everything. It's a lot more hectic because of it and there is some braindead +forwarding that does make the laning phase unenjoyable, but the push and pull between pairs per lane feels more interactive. There's an innate feeling that there's more possibility overall.

# ... but it's still a MOBA

I played 6v6 TF2 for many years, I'm used to calling and maincalling alongside my usual duties. That was always in a *leauge*. I *paid* to compete. Competition and legitimate practice scrims were places of effort separate from average gameplay. If I wanted to just work on something passively, a pub server was right there. I could goof off with no expectations.

It's my main gripe with fighting games: you have to show up at 100% because there is no "casuals only fast respawn" pub server, you are being put in the shit and you have to do the best you can do. Because Deadlock's a MOBA, every game is a scrim without the quality of a scrim: comms and pings, quality per player, general skill level balance isn't guaranteed at all and you get to sit through 30 minutes of it **at minimum**.

Clueless video game influencers crash out about the number of items in Deadlock. Items are easy: if you play a little and read a little, builds and counter items make sense. The real hurdle is all the unique MOBA-isms in Deadlock you either have to repurpose from prior MOBA experience or have to pick up specifically from Deadlock: 

- how to farm decently and consistently so you don't get left behind
- how to take advantage of all the resources on the map (bridge buffs)
- how to start incorporating bits and pieces of intermediate movement into your gameplay (i.e., sliding for infinite ammo, then progressing into using Fleetfoot for almost insta-slide spam)
- focusing certain characters during teamfights which means comms (which aren't guaranteed outside of a comp scrim/match)
- the *timing* for various item purchases, including counter items
- being aware of the constant need to push lanes
- grabbing the soul urn whenever you can if a carry on your team hasn't gotten it first

Then you have the varying quality in teammates, down to "are they mentally mature enough to play a MOBA online?" This makes playing standard/quick match a huge pain because not only are you likely to be subject to The Whiny Highschooler, you are **also** likely to be subjected to a real 3+ player stack in that same game.

# In closing

I think Deadlock is good. I think there's a lot of promise in the game. It still has too many MOBA-isms. The cast is so expressive and cool. The game is still not even being truly advertised by Valve, so much could change between now and when Valve finally starts trying to promote it publicly, so I don't have any truly concrete feelings about it. 

I don't really have any attachment to its competitive scene. I could be convinced to form a scrim team that's purely for scrims, but the nature of competition and scrims has changed so much: everything has a very blatant and literal pricetag associated with it. 

I'm not an influencer and the scene isn't as small as it is with something like TF2 or Quake, so it's not easy to become a familiar face who can build a community like that anymore. I'd love to have some kinda "Gr8Stalin's Scrim Squad" that spars with teams trying to play competitively and acts as a player incubator.

As long as I can DEagle Brand Dynamo, I'll be playing for a while. Interested to see how my opinions from here change the more I play and as the game changes and evolves.

If you care about the other parts of your life you won't really have the time to improve that skill at a rapid enough pace to keep up with the skill bracket you envision yourself or want to be at, but you'll improve at a steady cadence just by learning the gameplay and MOBA macro.
