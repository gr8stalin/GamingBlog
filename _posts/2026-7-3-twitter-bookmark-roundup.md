## Japan's FGC plays everything

<blockquote class="twitter-tweet" data-dnt="true" data-theme="dark"><p lang="en" dir="ltr">theres a big difference in the west, on how players here seem much more tribal (in a negative sense) when it comes to games they play. Japan's scene has always felt more interesting because they appear to have fewer public squabbles and a greater focus on team and small but numerous community events, which is probably a second-order effect of just wanting to play and give that same opportunity to others.

As someone who was both a "fuck the competitive sweat losers" public server casual and a "so tired of the pubbers who don't get it" competitive player at different points in my life, I try to be aware of my tribal aspects for gaming. While I haven't explored any of these deeply and they're mostly just conjecture from prior experiences, I think for the west:

### People are turned off by anime-leaning fighting game visuals

Engaging with Street Fighter, Mortal Kombat, and Tekken players, I've found that they're more "normie" leaning nerds. Their interaction with anime tends to be nonexistent or surface level, where mentions of it will earn anywhere from "I'm not into that shit" response to "Yo man I **love** [currently popular broad appeal shounen series]".

They'll have trouble either looking past or understanding the inspirations for various visual designs of characters in UNI2, Blazblue, or Guilty Gear; to the modern gamer anime designs are either ridiculous or comically sexualized (goonerbait).

This is typically enough to create a division by itself because to the "normie" leaning nerds, playing a game with anime stylization is weird and thus not worth engaging with. This may be a second-order effect of school-age socialization: interest in weird things makes you weird and a target for ridicule and/or ostracization.

### The fighting game played by an individual tends to be their fighting game identity

I think there are few out there who would ***really*** label themselves as genre enthusiasts. Everyone tends to have a series or two within the genre that they like the most in terms of design, visual and gameplay. Personally, tag fighters like DBFZ, Power Rangers BFTG, Skullgirls, etc. are cool to watch but I don't have the innate finesse for that kind of game and I don't have the patience (anymore) to spend weeks and weeks learning subgenres I'm not immediately interested in. I stick to what I can juggle in my life, which is another form of comfort.

Additionally, a singular game or series tends to be worn on an individual's sleeve as an aspect of their personality. I think tends to be where the most vocal lashouts against other games ("poverty" games) originate from.

### There's an ironic lack of community

For a group called the Fighting Game Community, I've noticed there isn't really a lot of it. There are things like streamer discords and there are obviously locals for the lucky, but while arcades were fading away in the 90s, PC gamers had been developing online communities for years.

By the 2000s, it was cheap and easy for a gaming clan to form: Invisionfree php forums had free tiers, sites like 50Webs offered what were basically free landing pages and guestbooks, XFire offered IM specifically for gamers, and console gaming would introduce party systems.

You don't really see clans in fighting games due to the 1v1 nature of the game, but I think a reason you see Japanese players able to play a multitude of fighting games is that they have the networks for it. They have established nooks and crannies for offline scenes and the JR enables easy access to them. I've talked about how [you have to be crazy to start a local]({% post_url 2026-2-24-you-gotta-be-crazy-to-start-a-local %}) in the west and I think anyone babbling about "support your locals" and "just go to your local" is completely unserious.

I think one of the reasons Street Fighter 6 has maintained its population is that it took the "lobby as interactive space" concept that anime fighters pioneered and implemented it successfully via the Battle Hub. It's a functioning community: you can go there and see the same names over and over again while encountering new ones consistently, there are lots of things to do in the Battle Hub between emulator cabinets, Extreme Battle, and avatar dress-up.

## Consumerism vs Consumerism, Japan

<blockquote class="twitter-tweet" data-dnt="true" data-theme="dark"><p lang="en" dir="ltr">do not have an audience for original manga work. There is no room for new original works to grow because then you waste a lot of money on table/printing, thus the fan art merch=sales cycle continues.<br><br>After experiencing comic markets in Japan, American anime cons cant compare.</p>&mdash; りにあ・かたや💖✨ 夏コミ日曜西ひ-47a (@KeikoKup) <a href="https://x.com/KeikoKup/status/1810598227894514163?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw">July 9, 2024</a></blockquote> <script async src="https://platform.x.com/widgets.js" charset="utf-8"></script> 

One thing that I've learned is that events like Comiket are a celebration of hobbyists. Anime/manga "parody" has sizable representation at Comiket but there's also indie games and music, not to mention books on trains, tanks, and fashion. Most prices at Comiket have built in limits of $5~$20 depending on the medium to both foster an environment of hobbyist creativity and to avoid conflict with media conglomerates that own the IPs that some panels are "parodying". Everyone is buying and consuming content but no one is getting rich at Comiket.

Anime cons in the west are more about consumerism. Everyone wants to own some item or artwork of the series they're currently consuming so they feel like a "real fan", and this group that desires to be a "real fan" becomes the outset market for every artist at a western convention. With the vice grip that indistinguishable Hoyoslop characters from Zenless Genshin Waves and indistinguishable VTubers have on anime, artists who want to make their own thing (in this case, original creation) have to have a preexisting fanbase to drive both sales and curiosity from con attendees.

At Otakon 2025, there was a massive falloff in interesting import content and a spike in plastic crap and whatever's popular at the moment. For 2025, Umamusume finally made it overseas so all the booths stocked up on Umamusume stuff.

The markup wasn't crazy and I was willing to pay the "con tax" on an out-of-production prize fig of Gold Ship, but I've been seeing the Anime Expo posts for 2026 and Umamusume stuff there is getting huge markups in price: stuff that'd normally be $25~$30 if you bought it in Japan getting marked up to $135+.

Additionally, there were a lot of booths with cheap ABS plastic molds of weapons from Halo, Gears of War, Warhammer 40k, and others. A *lot*, to the point where you could move between rows of booths and see another booth with the same ABS plastic weaponry.

## Unseriousness

<blockquote class="twitter-tweet" data-dnt="true" data-theme="dark"><p lang="en" dir="ltr">This is called being unserious and you will continue to be crushed by the weight of both your ambitions and your inadequacy until you choose to address at least one <a href="https://t.co/Omymh0cURn">https://t.co/Omymh0cURn</a></p>&mdash; Kondé Oko (@kondeoko) <a href="https://x.com/kondeoko/status/1818276542583296004?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw">July 30, 2024</a></blockquote> <script async src="https://platform.x.com/widgets.js" charset="utf-8"></script> 

This is a shorter one. Despite this post being two years old, the term "unserious" has stuck with me as an excellent way to describe a certain type of person with one word. Those who are unserious:

- Have a lot to say about a given topic, but don't have sufficient experience with it
- Have grand ambitious and fantasies of fame and adoration, but don't have the attitude and work ethic required

I think this shares territory with the repurposing of the acronym LARP as a way to describe someone pretending to like or do something for the social recognition and benefits associated with that something.
